The tool reads one loan request and returns two separate results: an ex ante ESG rating and a forecast social return on investment (SROI). Every step is shown, every number in the parameter table carries its source and status, and the whole assessment can be downloaded as a passport that anyone can replay.

SROI

The method uses the impact adjustments and discounting of the Guide to SROI (Social Value International), with stated departures and exclusions. Its ratio is a partial, illustrative benefit-cost scenario, not a full SROI; the exclusions are listed at the end of this page. It does not do the Guide's stakeholder and materiality stages, which a real assessment needs: the outcomes here are set in advance, not defined with the people affected. And its central result is a lifetime scenario: the yearly benefit is counted over the facility's life, longer than the outcome periods SROI studies usually evidence. So the result also shows SROI with benefits counted for only 1 and 5 years, each where it is shorter than the period, divided by the same inputs as the full period (the project cost and the running cost over the whole period): only the benefits stop. It is a check on the period, not a result at the Guide's scope.

People's yearly value is the number of regular adult participants in the first full year at the facility (people, not visits) times a value per person. It takes deadweight (those who would have taken part anyway, so the count entered is before that deduction), displacement (outcomes lost elsewhere because of the project) and attribution (the share of the outcomes due to other programmes or organisations; capital co-funders are already counted in the project cost), then loses a drop-off share each later year. Drop-off stands for both outcomes that fade and fewer people reached; running costs are not reduced with it, which errs low. Deadweight and displacement do not overlap: deadweight is everyone who would have been active or engaged anyway, including people who simply move from another facility; displacement is outcomes lost elsewhere beyond them, for example people who stop taking part when another facility loses users or income and cuts its offer. The avoided-carbon value is the project's own yearly saving times a value per tonne; the saving entered is gross, measured or engineered against the building before the works; a new facility counts no carbon saving: carbon benefits and added emissions of new facilities, replacements of an existing building included, are not modelled, a stated exclusion rather than a finding; it then takes its own deadweight (the share minimum energy rules or a cleaner grid would have saved anyway; central 0 assumes neither would have; ranged to 50 percent) and attribution, and no displacement or drop-off. One attribution share is used for people and carbon alike, a simplification. The method does not value any harm the project may cause; a real assessment would list them. Both are discounted over the benefit period and divided by all inputs: the total project cost plus the present value of the yearly running cost the project adds. The loan alone is never the denominator, so the lender's share is never credited with the whole project's value.

Values per person are benefit transfers from published UK evidence for adults: an adult active 150 minutes or more a week, and an adult aged 30 to 49 engaging with culture every few months. They are marked "transferred": they show the order of magnitude of a recognised method, not a measured Italian value. For sport the two ends of the range are two activity levels; for culture they are two different models for adults aged 30 to 49 (general health from engagement every few months; mental health from participation once a week or more). Neither is uncertainty around one estimate. Each central value sits at the top of its range, so draws of these two values only go down. Carbon deadweight's central 0 is the bottom of its range, so its draws only go up, and carbon benefits fall as it rises; the carbon value's central EUR 208 is the bottom of its range too, so its draws only go up, and carbon benefits rise with it. At the method's own values, the first three pull the assumption range down against the central result, the carbon value pulls it up, and other assumptions draw both ways, so the central result can lie outside the range. A reviewer's value can change these directions: a value per person set to 0, for example, is drawn upwards only, towards more value. When the central result lies outside the range, the result card says so and names each assumption drawn one way only, read from the values in use, with the way its draws move the SROI ratio: towards a higher SROI, towards a lower one, or both where the ratio rises at some values sampled and falls at others; a higher SROI is not always more total value, since a draw can lower both the benefits and the costs counted; some values sampled may leave it unchanged. An assumption that moves the result only when the other one-sided assumptions are at the middle of their ranges, for example because another sets a part of the value to zero, is named and marked so. Where an assumption moves the result one way with the others at their values in use and another way with the other one-sided assumptions at the middle of their ranges, the card shows both readings. The directions are those found at the values sampled: every whole year for a period, since a period counts whole years only, and otherwise 96 steps and two points right beside the value in use. The ages each value covers differ. The sport value is for adults aged 16 or over, older adults included, so it does not support children under 16. The culture values are for adults aged 30 to 49, so they do not support anyone younger or older. The low end of a range is not an age adjustment: leave people outside those ages out of the users entered until a value is justified for their age group.

Ranges, sensitivity and break-even

One number hides how much it rests on assumptions. The tool shows three things beside it. The assumption range draws every ranged assumption 2,000 times at once (triangular distributions, fixed seed), the benefit period included, and reports the 10th to 90th percentile. A reviewer's value is the peak of its draws, across its published range widened to hold it. So a deadweight of 100 percent removes participation from the central result, but the draws still take deadweight below 100 percent, down to the bottom of its published range, and so count some participation the central result leaves out; the result card says so. The distributions are themselves assumptions, so the range shows sensitivity, not statistical confidence or likelihood. The sensitivity bars move one assumption alone across its whole stated range (every whole year for a benefit period) and span the lowest to the highest SROI seen, widest first. The break-even list gives, for each assumption, each value at which a fine scan finds SROI crossing 1.0, each marked inside or outside the stated range, and for users the number needed for 1.0. A crossing is inside when the stated range holds values on both sides of it, one below 1.0 and one at or above it; a ratio of exactly 1.0 counts as at or above, in the list and in the verdict alike. For a benefit period, a first year on the far side of 1.0 (at or above it, or below it) that lies in the range, reached from a year below the range, is marked as a step that starts outside it. A reviewer's value outside an assumption's range can leave that whole range below 1.0 with no crossing inside it. The list says the range is below 1.0 only when every value scanned in it, the value in use included, is; when the scan finds values below 1.0 and values at or above 1.0 in the range but cannot place the point where SROI reaches 1.0, it says that instead. In both cases the result is not called robust. Values inside the range are those whose evidence matters most. A ratio that only grazes 1.0 between two scan steps can be missed, so the list is what the scan finds, not proof that no other value crosses. A benefit period counts whole years, so its break-even is the first whole year on the far side of 1.0 (at or above 1.0 going up, below 1.0 going down); with a running cost, a longer period can bring SROI below 1.0 again, and both crossings are listed.

Regional participation gap beside return

The regional participation gap is part of the ESG rating (indicator S2), never a multiplier inside SROI. A low regional rate of sport practice or cultural participation is a gap, not proof of unmet need for a facility, so an efficient project and one that reaches an underserved area can be compared openly. The portfolio view plots each loan by gap and by return.

ESG rating

Twelve indicators, four per pillar, each scored from 0 to 100 by a stated rule, shown beside its points in the step table. Pillar scores are weighted averages of their indicators; the total weights the pillars and maps to a band from A (80 and over) to E (under 35). The rating is compensatory: a high score on one indicator or pillar can make up for a low one, such as a high physical climate risk, and no pillar has a minimum. The weights and bands are this prototype's own, published here so they can be challenged; no public framework prescribes them. The indicator themes draw on the ICMA Social Bond Principles and the EIB Environmental and Social Standards. Outputs use IRIS+ terms: facility type under PD7557 Community Facilities Type (which names arts and other cultural facilities and community sports facilities), people reached as PI4060 Client Individuals: Total (unique people, not foot traffic). Indicator S1 (participants per EUR 100,000 of cost) counts only the participants entered in the users field, the valuation-eligible adults (sport adults 16 or over; culture adults aged 30 to 49). Children, and culture participants of other ages, are left out of S1 as well as of SROI, so S1 is not a total of people reached and understates it where those groups take part. The same users count enters both S1 and SROI, so an overstated count can raise both the rating and the return; the page cannot check it, and only the project's own evidence can.

Reviewers, passports and replay

A reviewer can move any assumption with a slider. The change reruns the assessment and is recorded beside the method's own value. The passport holds every input, every value with its unit, source and status, and the headline results (rating, pillar scores, SROI, its range and the users needed for 1.0) as canonical JSON; the steps, charts and break-even list follow from these. A loan opened from the synthetic book keeps its id and synthetic flag in the passport while the form still matches it. Its SHA-256 fingerprint shows whether a copy differs from a kept original. Loading a passport first checks it: every value must be the method's own published value unless it is marked as a reviewer's, and a reviewer's value must lie in its allowed range. Only then is it rerun with this method version, to say whether its results follow from its inputs: a consistency check, not proof of who made it. Two passports, for example from two reviewers, can be compared field by field without either overwriting the other.

What this prototype is not

It is not a credit decision, not a certified rating and not a copy or estimate of any lender's own platform, whose weights are not public. Values marked illustrative are placeholders that show how the method works. The portfolio view uses an invented loan book, labelled synthetic. The ratio is not the project's complete impact: it leaves out donated resources and volunteer time, any harm the project causes, and outcomes beyond the two values per person and carbon. Leaving them out is not a finding that they are small: their materiality is not assessed. There is no construction period: for every purpose the project cost falls at the start and benefits begin in year 1, so a project that takes years to build has its benefits counted too early. Savings on energy bills are not counted as a benefit, and the running cost entered cannot be below zero, so a project that lowers running costs gets no credit for it. For energy works the values per person still count: participation value falls as deadweight rises, and 100 percent deadweight removes it whatever the other inputs, which the deadweight note advises for works on an open facility that bring no additional participation and avoid no loss of participation (accessibility works can add participation without adding capacity); the assumption range still draws deadweight below 100 percent, so it counts some participation the central result leaves out; the carbon saving is valued on its own. Not in this version: a note of the project's own evidence carried in the passport; two separate culture scenarios, one for each published value, in place of one range between them; and a benefit period entered on the loan form apart from the facility's life. A real use would need stakeholder input and project evidence.
